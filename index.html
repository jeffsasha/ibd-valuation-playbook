01 / THE ROLE

What an IBD team actually does

“Investment banking” is not investing the bank’s own money. It is advice and execution: helping corporate, sponsor, and government clients make high-stakes financing and transaction decisions.

Mergers & acquisitions

Advise a buyer or seller on valuation, deal structure, negotiation, diligence, and process strategy.

OUTPUT → PITCHBOOK, VALUATION, PROCESS MATERIALS

Equity capital markets

Help companies raise equity through IPOs, follow-ons, or private placements while framing the investor story.

OUTPUT → OFFERING ANALYSIS, INVESTOR MATERIALS

Debt & leveraged finance

Design and arrange loans or bonds, balancing borrowing capacity, cost, covenants, and rating impact.

OUTPUT → DEBT CAPACITY, TERMS, CREDIT METRICS

Restructuring & special situations

Advise companies or creditors when the capital structure is stressed and outcomes depend on liquidity and recovery.

OUTPUT → LIQUIDITY, RECOVERY, SCENARIO ANALYSIS

04 / DCF DEEP DIVE

DCF, FCF & WACC—from first principle to output

Use this as the complete mental model: forecast the cash generated by operations, discount it at the return required by capital providers, then bridge from the value of operations to the value attributable to common shareholders.

Forecast operating cash→ Discount each cash flow→ Add terminal value→ Enterprise value→ Bridge to equity value
THE FULL MODEL

What a DCF is actually doing

01

A DCF says a business is worth the present value of the future cash its operations can distribute to capital providers. It is an intrinsic valuation: the answer comes from operating assumptions, not another company’s trading multiple.

EV = Σ UFCFt / (1 + WACC)t + Terminal Value / (1 + WACC)n
  1. 01
    Choose the valuation date and forecast horizon

    Set the date from which cash is discounted. Forecast until the company reaches a reasonably steady operating state—often five to ten years, but longer for immature or cyclical businesses.

  2. 02
    Build the operating forecast

    Project revenue from volume, price, customers, market share, or business-unit drivers. Translate revenue into margins, taxes, reinvestment, and working-capital needs.

  3. 03
    Calculate unlevered free cash flow

    Start with EBIT after tax, add back non-cash D&A, subtract capital expenditures, and subtract the investment in net working capital.

  4. 04
    Estimate WACC

    Blend the required returns of equity and debt using market-value capital weights. Match the discount rate to unlevered cash flow and to the currency and risk of the forecast.

  5. 05
    Discount the explicit cash flows

    Each year’s cash flow is worth less today. Use year-end discounting unless the model specifies a mid-year convention for cash generated throughout the year.

  6. 06
    Estimate and discount terminal value

    Capture cash flows after the explicit forecast with perpetual growth or an exit multiple. Terminal value is measured at the end of the final forecast year, then discounted back.

  7. 07
    Sum to enterprise value

    Present value of forecast-period UFCF plus present value of terminal value equals the value of core operations to debt and equity capital providers.

  8. 08
    Bridge to equity value and per-share value

    Subtract debt and debt-like claims, add cash and non-operating assets, then divide by diluted shares. Use fully diluted shares, not merely basic shares.

Interview answer in one breath

“Forecast unlevered free cash flow, discount it at WACC, add the present value of terminal value to get enterprise value, bridge to equity value, and divide by diluted shares.”

THE NUMERATOR

Free cash flow: profit converted into distributable cash

02

A standard enterprise-value DCF uses unlevered free cash flow (UFCF or FCFF): cash generated by operations before interest and debt repayment, available to all capital providers.

UFCF = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate) + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
EBIT × (1 − T)NOPAT

Operating profit after tax as if the company had no debt. Use taxes on EBIT—not reported tax expense distorted by interest or one-offs.

+ D&AAdd back non-cash expense

D&A reduced EBIT but did not use current-period cash. Add it back here; do not confuse the add-back with “free money.”

− CapexFund the asset base

Cash spent on property, equipment, software, or capitalized development. Separate maintenance from growth capex when the business story calls for it.

− ΔNWCFund day-to-day operations

An increase in operating working capital consumes cash; a decrease releases cash. Usually exclude cash, debt, and other financing items.

THE DISCOUNT RATE

WACC: the opportunity cost of funding the business

03

Weighted average cost of capital blends the required return demanded by equity investors and lenders. It is not simply “the company’s borrowing rate.”

WACC = [E / (D + E)] × Re + [D / (D + E)] × Rd × (1 − T)
E / (D + E)

Equity weight

Use market value of equity. For a public company, share price × diluted shares is the usual starting point.

D / (D + E)

Debt weight

Use the market value of interest-bearing debt when available; book value may be a practical approximation.

Re

Cost of equity

Usually estimated with CAPM: risk-free rate + levered beta × equity risk premium, plus any justified country or size premium.

Rd × (1 − T)

After-tax cost of debt

Use the current marginal borrowing cost, not the historical coupon. The tax shield lowers the effective cost when interest is deductible.

CAPMRe = Rf + βL × Equity Risk Premium

Risk-free rate: match currency and duration. Beta: operating and financial risk relative to the market. ERP: incremental return expected for bearing market equity risk.

Match the numerator

UFCF → WACC. FCFE → cost of equity. Nominal cash flow → nominal rate. Same currency, inflation basis, and risk profile.

Use target structure

WACC should reflect the long-run capital structure supporting forecast cash flows, not a temporarily unusual financing mix.

Keep it circularity-aware

Capital structure affects beta and WACC, while value affects capital weights. Bankers often use target weights or an iterative solution.

THE ENDGAME

Terminal value, present value & the equity bridge

04

Terminal value captures all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast. Because it often represents a large share of enterprise value, it must be economically defensible and sensitivity-tested.

PERPETUAL GROWTH

Gordon Growth method

TVn = UFCFn × (1 + g) / (WACC − g)

Assumes cash flow grows forever at a stable rate. WACC must exceed g. Use a mature growth rate consistent with long-run nominal economic growth and reinvestment needs.

EXIT MULTIPLE

Market multiple method

TVn = Final-year metric × Selected multiple

Usually applies EV/EBITDA or EV/EBIT to a normalized final-year metric. Justify the multiple with trading evidence and check the implied perpetual growth rate.

Present-value discipline
PV(UFCFt) = UFCFt / (1 + WACC)tPV(TV) = TVn / (1 + WACC)n

Use t − 0.5 rather than t under a mid-year convention. For a partial first year, use the exact stub period. Keep the terminal-value date aligned with the final projected period.

TERMINAL CHECKImplied exit multiple

Under Gordon Growth, divide terminal value by the final-year metric. Compare with mature peers.

MARKET CHECKImplied perpetual growth

Under an exit multiple, solve for g. An implausible result may reveal an aggressive multiple.

VALUE CHECKTerminal-value share

A very high share is not automatically wrong, but it means assumptions beyond the forecast drive the answer.

SENSITIVITY CHECKWACC × growth / multiple

Present a range, not a point estimate. Explain which operating assumptions create the movement.

Eight mistakes to catch before the model leaves your desk
  • WACC ≤ perpetual growth
  • Interest deducted in UFCF
  • D&A added back but capex omitted
  • Working-capital signs reversed
  • Terminal value not discounted
  • Mixed currency or nominal/real inputs
  • Basic shares used instead of diluted
  • Debt, cash, or leases double-counted

06 / INTERVIEW LENS

Explain the “why,” not just the formula

A strong analyst can build the model. A strong candidate can also explain which model fits the question, where it can fail, and how its assumptions connect to the business.

Technical questions to rehearse

A buyer usually pays for control and may share some expected synergies with the seller. Precedents can also reflect different market conditions, which is why transaction selection and timing matter.

08 / QUICK REFERENCE

The language of valuation

These terms recur across nearly every interview, model, and deal discussion.

ENTERPRISE VALUE

Value of core operations to all capital providers.

EQUITY VALUE

Value attributable to common shareholders.

WACC

Blended required return of debt and equity investors.

UNLEVERED FCF

Cash flow before debt payments, available to all investors.

CONTROL PREMIUM

Extra value paid to obtain decision-making control.

SYNERGY

Incremental value from combining businesses.

ACCRETION

Increase in the buyer’s pro forma EPS after a deal.

IRR

Annualized return that sets investment NPV to zero.

Valuation is a range, not a verdict.

The real skill is connecting assumptions to the business, triangulating methods, and communicating what changes the answer.
